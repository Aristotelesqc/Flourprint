## What was measured

### Table 2 of the 2015 FRI paper (p. 42): total CO2 after 180 min, by yeast and dose

Rheofermentometer, 315 g dough, 30 C. Dose in g of **dry matter** of the yeast product per 100 g
of flour. cfu are per 100 g of flour in the table (the text on p. 39 says "per 100 g of dough";
the two are printed differently).

| Dose | CY cfu       | CY CO2 (ml) | IDY cfu     | IDY CO2 (ml) |
| ---- | ------------ | ----------- | ----------- | ------------ |
| 0.25 | 3.11 x 10^9  | 740 +/- 30  | 1.72 x 10^8 | 720 +/- 10   |
| 0.50 | 6.21 x 10^9  | 1100 +/- 10 | 3.43 x 10^8 | 1040 +/- 10  |
| 0.75 | 9.29 x 10^9  | 1440 +/- 11 | 5.13 x 10^8 | 1450 +/- 10  |
| 1.00 | 1.24 x 10^10 | 1410 +/- 30 | 6.83 x 10^8 | 1370 +/- 61  |

| 3.00 | 3.67 x 10^10 | 1490 +/- 40 | 2.02 x 10^9 | 1530 +/- 80  |

At equal dry-matter dose the total CO2 over 180 min is the same within the printed spread, from
0.75 on a plateau near 1400 to 1500 ml (p. 41: "the CO2 volume changed only slightly" above
0.75 g/100 g flour). The IDY cfu are about one eighteenth of the CY cfu at every dose (computed
here from the table: 18.1 at 0.25 and at 0.75, 18.2 at 3.00); the text says "lower cfu value than
CY by approximately ten-fold" (p. 40). The maximum gas formation rate was higher for IDY at the top
dose: "the vCO2 of 1630 ml/h was significantly higher than for CY with 1260 ml/h" (p. 59, dose
not stated in the sentence; plotted against cfu in Fig. 3A, p. 41, not digitised). "a period of
at least 45 min was required" to reach the maximum rate, and above 0.75 g/100 g flour (CY 9.29 x
10^9, IDY 5.13 x 10^8 cfu/100 g flour) the time to reach it no longer shortened much (p. 41).

### Gas retention and structure (2014 LWT, dry yeast, 30 C, 135 min; 2015 FRI)

- Time of porosity Tx (gas first escapes) for 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 g dry yeast per 100 g flour:
  **142, 99, 60 and 49 min** (p. 32). Density at that moment: 350.52 +/- 25.03, 318.04 +/- 14.61,
  310.55 +/- 34.41, 284.79 +/- 47.97 kg/m3, "no significant differences between the samples"
  (p. 32), average 311.5 +/- 35.93.
- "the kinetics of CO2-synthesis does not affect the gas retention capacity, which therefore is
  exclusively limited by the amount of gas and the stability of the dough matrix" (p. 33, 2014
  LWT, dry yeast). The 2015 FRI paper qualifies this by yeast form (next two bullets).
- IDY: the CO2 at Tx "remained at an almost constant level between 538 and 564 ml" per 315 g dough
  over the dose range (p. 42). CY: the CO2 at Tx fell with the gas formation rate, to "a minimum
  of 372 ml per 315 g sample" (p. 60); the correlation of Tx and CO2-at-Tx with vCO2 is printed
  for CY (R2 0.8977) and IDY (R2 0.3383) in the Fig. 4 caption (p. 42).
- "Above a concentration of 0.25 g of dry yeast matter per 100 g of flour, at some point during
  fermentation more CO2 was produced than the dough matrix was able to absorb" (p. 41).
- Gas volume fraction after kneading up to 23 % (CY) and 26 % (IDY); unyeasted 6 to 9 %; "the
  gas level rose to 83 % for both yeast types" (pp. 40, 44).
- Dough weakening, 2014 (p. 36): complex shear modulus |G*| reduced by "20-50 %" by yeast; loss
  factor tan delta of the unyeasted dough 0.35 to 0.39 over 135 min; "up to 57 % higher loss
  factors immediately after kneading" with yeast (pp. 29, 36); tan delta after kneading rises
  "asymptotically from 0.36 to 0.55 with linear rising yeast concentrations" (p. 58). pH of the
  unyeasted dough stays 5.80 +/- 0.06; yeasted doughs fall to 5.33 +/- 0.03 (p. 32). For CY at
  high gas formation rate "the dough pH decreased down to 5.1" (p. 42).

### Specific bread volume by yeast form (2015 FRI, pp. 42-44)

Loaves from 250 g dough, proofed at 30 C and 80 % relative humidity, fermentation time set by
rheofermentometer characteristic times (T0, T1, T'1, T'2, T2, Tx, Tv) of each dose (p. 39).

- CY: the highest CO2 production (1190 ml; density 0.264 g/ml; 79.8 % gas) was with **2 %**
  after 180 min; "the highest bread volume (3.9 ml/g; density: 0.257 g/ml) resulted from
  fermentation with **1.5 %** of yeast until reaching T1 (116 min)" (p. 42). Above 1.0 g/100 g
  flour a critical CO2 volume of about 1100 ml made the matrix unstable; up to 0.5 g/100 g flour no
  collapse (p. 42). The lowest CY dose "0.5%" needed three times the fermentation time of the 2.5
  and 3 % doses to reach the same gas volume, and "the loaf volume was significantly lower at 3.1
  ml/g rather than 3.5 ml/g" (p. 43).
- IDY: "The maximum specific bread volume of IDY leavened products was approximately 3.3 ml/g,
  which is considerably lower than that produced with compressed yeast (3.9 ml/g)" (p. 42); the
  conclusion prints "the maximum value, 3.28 ml/g, was considerably lower than with CY at 3.88
  ml/g" (p. 44). A critical destabilising gas content of "approximately 600 ml" is stated for IDY
  (p. 42). Oven rise of IDY loaves was "reduced by increasing the initial specific dough volume"
  and became negative at specific dough volumes near 3 to 4 ml/g (p. 43, Fig. 6B, figure only).
- "the Rheofermentometer data could not be used to predict the specific bread volume" (p. 43).

### Glutathione from dead cells (2015 Food Chem., pp. 46-50)

- 6 fresh and 21 dry commercial yeasts (the text counts 15 dry product names, with repeat
  batches): dead cells 1.77 to 8.82 % in fresh and 7.42 to 75.14 % in dry yeast; water-soluble
  glutathione (GSH) 1.16 to 10.51 mg/g yeast dry basis in fresh and 5.37 to 81.22 mg/g in dry;
  R2 0.8483 between dead cells and GSH (pp. 47, 48).
- Two batches of the same product differed significantly: relative deviation up to 75 % in dead
  cells and up to 91 % in GSH (p. 48, "data not shown"). "the production process of fresh and dry
  yeast is subjected to fluctuations which strongly influence the quality of the final product"
  (p. 48).
- Yeast-equivalent GSH (7.3, 27.8, 58.6, 76.2 mg per 100 g flour for 1 g dry matter yeast per 100
  g flour) in unleavened dough: development time 7.4 +/- 0.57 min (no GSH) to 2.5 +/- 0.17 min
  (76.2 mg); stability to 1.8 +/- 0.10 min; softening 282 +/- 13.57 FU; |G*| 8170 +/- 570 to
  3550 +/- 780 Pa; tan delta 0.39 +/- 0.01 to 0.55 +/- 0.03; the effect is asymptotic (p. 48).
  "GSH concentrations equally to the amount in fresh yeast sample do not significantly affect the
  rheological dough properties" (p. 48).
- Chemical leavening (9.9 g SAPP 10 + 7.2 g NaHCO3 per 100 g flour; 666 +/- 30 ml CO2 in 180 min,
  "close to the typical CO2 volume (700 ml) produced by 0.5 g dry yeast per 100 g flour", p. 49)
  with added GSH: weakening coefficient (loss of dough height) 0.25 +/- 0.07 % rising to 5.95 +/-
  0.21 % within 0 to 58.6 mg GSH, and 20.4 +/- 1.56 % at 76.2 mg; no gas escape within that
  interval (p. 49). The thesis discussion adds that with a GDL system GSH did not reduce gas
  retention and "the aeration of the dough matrix was overall improved", while the specific bread
  volume was "significantly impaired" (pp. 63, 64).

## The "21 % lower specific bread volume with instant dry yeast" statement

- **Where it is printed:** once, in the abstract of the 2015 _Food Research International_
  article, reproduced at thesis p. 38 (journal p. 860): "Although the dough leavened with IDY
  showed improved extensibility during fermentation, the specific bread volume was 21% below that
  of the samples leavened with CY." Read from a 300 dpi enlargement. No other occurrence of 21 %
  in sections 2.2 to 2.4, the summary or the discussion.
- **Under what conditions:** both yeasts at equal **dry-matter** doses (0.25 to 3.0 g per 100 g of
  flour), flour type 550, 58.6 g water per 100 g flour less the yeast water, 30 C kneading and
  fermentation, loaves from 250 g dough proofed at 80 % relative humidity for times set by
  rheofermentometer characteristic times, 230 C bake (pp. 39, 40). The comparison is between the
  **best loaf of each yeast across doses and fermentation endpoints**, not between the two forms
  at one dose and one time.
- **The body does not print 21 %.** It prints approximately 3.3 against 3.9 ml/g (p. 42), 3.28
  against 3.88 ml/g (p. 44) and 3.3 against 3.9 ml/g (p. 61). Computed here, 3.28/3.88 is a
  15.5 % reduction and 3.3/3.9 is 15.4 %. The 21 % of the abstract cannot be reproduced from the
  printed maxima; no derivation of it is printed. The thesis summary (p. 1) says only that CY
  "had a significantly higher loaf volume".

## Fresh-to-dry equivalence: what the thesis prints

- No conversion factor between compressed and dry yeast product mass is derived or tested. The
  design **fixes equal dry matter** (p. 39), so product masses differ by each product's moisture,
  which is not printed for these two products.
- General statements in the introduction (p. 12), not measurements of these products: compressed
  yeast "with a dry matter content of 28-33%"; dry yeast with "less than 10%" water.
- At equal dry matter the 180-min total CO2 is the same for CY and IDY (Table 2, above); the gas
  formation rate and the loaf outcome are not (above). "the total CO2-volume of approximately
  1500 ml per 315 g sample, however, was identical for both trading forms" (p. 59). The thesis
  attributes the faster IDY rate to higher vitality, with trehalose, glycogen or zymase from lysed
  cells named as candidates (p. 59: "seems", "seems reasonable", "plausible"); these are
  hypotheses, not measurements.
- cfu: IDY has a tenth to a twentieth of the CY cfu at equal dry matter (about 1/18 from Table 2) yet the same total gas; the
  authors read this as "higher yeast activity in IDY" (p. 40) and note "manufacturers have been
  able to standardize both products well" (p. 44).

## What it does not establish for Flourprint

- **No conversion factor.** Nothing printed supports or contradicts the assumed 1/0.33 (fresh from
  active dry) or 1/0.40 (fresh from instant) mass ratios; the dose is dry matter, the moisture of
  the products is not printed, and the doses are far above home doses.
- **No lean home dough at low dose.** The dose range is 0.25 to 3.0 g dry matter per 100 g flour
  (CY product mass roughly 0.8 to 10 g per 100 g flour if 28 to 33 % dry matter is assumed, which
  is the introduction's general range, not this CY's moisture). Most outputs are at 0.5 % or more.
  The lowest point, 0.25 g, still finished a 180-min gas test.
- **No cold.** All fermentations are at a controlled 30 C. No refrigerated step, no room
  temperature, no dough temperature after kneading printed.
- **No readiness endpoint.** The rheofermentometer times (T1, Tx) are instrument endpoints of a
  small closed-chamber test, not "ready to bake" times; the paper says they could not predict
  specific bread volume (p. 43).
- **A single laboratory, one flour type per paper, one German commercial source per yeast
  form.** Batch-to-batch deviation of the same product (up to 75 % dead cells, 91 % GSH) is
  larger than most dose effects Flourprint models. The dough formulas differ between the three
  papers and salt, sugar and fat are not printed for the one that compares CY and IDY.
- **Glutathione is shown as a likely candidate, not proved for yeast-leavened dough.** The
  effect was measured with added GSH in unleavened and chemically leavened dough; the authors
  write that it "might be responsible" (p. 36), "a very likely candidate" (p. 49), and that the
  weakening interaction with CO2 "has to be investigated" (p. 65). The IDY-GSH link to loaf volume
  is a hypothesis in the thesis.
- **Printed inconsistencies, kept as printed:** cfu "per 100 g of dough" (p. 39) against "per 100 g
  of flour" (Table 2, p. 42); ascorbic acid "20 g/100 kg flour" (p. 30); 60.3 against 60.4 g
  water per 100 g flour (p. 30); abstract 21 % against body 15 % (above); Table 2 of the Food
  Chem. paper prints 176 and 0.8, 40.9 and 0.1 without a plus-minus sign (p. 49).
