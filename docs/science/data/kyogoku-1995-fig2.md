## Other numbers in the paper (text and Table 1)

- After 7 days at 0, 2.5 and 5 °C, the parent's gas production at 30 °C fell to 86, 74 and 10 % of
  unrefrigerated dough (243.0 mL/g yeast/2 h). The parent dough "expanded greatly" in storage: its
  volume after 3 days at 2.5 °C was several times that of the mutant's.
- Table 1, white bread dough (2 % yeast, 5 % sugar, 2 % salt) stored 7 days at 5 °C: gas at 30 °C
  343.8 before and 120.0 mL/g yeast/2 h after (ratio 0.349); bread specific volume 3.2 mL/g.

## What it does and does not show

- It is the first dough measurement in this directory below 15 °C. The parent's 5 °C gas is 3.0 %
  of its 20 °C gas (DERIVED, 3.31/109.7). The shipped Arrhenius law (`Ea` 98.3 kJ/mol) gives
  11.4 % for the same pair, and 53 kJ/mol gives 31 %.
- Whether the curve bends depends on the 5 °C bar. The second Opus report on #33 (its fits,
  checked here only for consistency) finds one constant `Ea` fitting 10–30 °C (85 kJ/mol) about as
  well as a square-root form, and 20–35 °C at 55 kJ/mol; only 5–30 °C rejects a constant `Ea`, and
  only if the 5 °C bar is taken at face value. A square-root fit to 5–30 °C extrapolates to zero
  near 1.7 °C, but gas was recorded at 0 and 2.5 °C, so this is a shape parameter, not a threshold.
- Limits: 3 % wet yeast and 15 % sugar (a sweet dough, outside the shipped domain); one strain, one
  lab, no replicates stated; a 2-h total of evolved gas (with water and ethanol vapour), not a
  production rate. Two biases of opposite sign are each about as large as the cold signal: a warm
  start (no equilibration after the 30 °C hold) raises the cold bars, and CO2 dissolving into
  colder dough lowers them. It does not, on its own, justify a contract change.
